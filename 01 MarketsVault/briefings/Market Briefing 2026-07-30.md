## 2. Theme Tracker

### 2.1 fed-path — US Fed path & inflation — TURNING (from a coin-flip hike debate to a genuine credibility crisis)
- **What changed:** The FOMC held the fed funds target range at 3.50-3.75% by a 9-3 vote — Cleveland's Beth Hammack, Minneapolis's Neel Kashkari and Dallas's Lorie Logan all dissented in favor of a 25bp hike, the largest same-direction dissent bloc since 2016. Chair Kevin Warsh, in his second press conference since taking office in May, leaned hawkish on language ("there is no soft inflation target," "five-plus years of inflation above target cannot be cured in nine weeks") while offering no clear signal on the path ahead — he has deliberately shortened forward guidance since taking the chair. When pressed on why the Fed didn't hike given the dissent, Warsh pointed to the market-driven jump in long-end yields as already "doing some of the Fed's work." Coverage broadly framed the outcome as leaving investors "baffled" rather than reassured. Wall Street's own read: a hike now looks more likely at the next meeting, given three sitting officials are already on record wanting one.
- **Market effect:** The 10-year Treasury yield rose to roughly 4.66-4.68%; the 30-year spiked to about 5.20-5.21%, its highest level since 2007. The 2-year yield actually fell about 4bp to 4.24% — a bear steepener. The S&P 500 fell 1.51% to 7,316 and the Nasdaq Composite fell 1.74% to 24,442, both erasing an earlier intraday rally that briefly turned green during Warsh's remarks before selling off into the close.
- **Concept:** A "bear steepener" is when long-end yields rise while the short end holds or falls — the opposite of what you'd expect if the market thought the Fed was about to tighten more aggressively soon. The front end tracks near-term policy expectations (still anchored near a hold), while the long end tracks the market's own independent view of inflation and growth over many years. When the long end sells off on a hold, not a hike, it usually means the market has stopped trusting the central bank's inflation-fighting credibility and is pricing in a higher inflation-risk premium itself — effectively doing the tightening the Fed declined to do. That's a more worrying signal than a simple rate move, because it's a statement about trust, not just about the next 25bp.

### 2.2 hormuz-oil — Hormuz / Iran war — STRENGTHENING (the war widened geographically for the first time)
- **What changed:** Overnight, Iran struck two new locations it had not previously hit: a Chinese-owned building in northern Kuwait (killing one worker) and, in a first for this war, a US-owned gas storage tanker at Egypt's Mediterranean port of Damietta via drone. Jordan intercepted five further missiles Thursday morning; the IRGC vowed further retaliation. The US and Saudi militaries struck back broadly inside Iran — Qeshm Island, Kish Island, Bushehr, Khuzestan, Hormozgan and Fars province — with a residential strike on Qeshm killing a family of three. Diplomatically, Iran's Deputy FM confirmed Tehran rejected Oman's proposal to split Hormuz shipping lanes 50/50 with a Malacca Strait-style voluntary fee, countering instead with a plan to route all traffic through Iranian waters alone — a proposal Oman and the US consider insufficient. Saudi Arabia separately announced a 14-nation defensive coalition (Kuwait, Qatar, Bahrain, Pakistan, Turkey, Egypt, Jordan) to protect Red Sea shipping; Oman and the UAE are notably absent. - **Market effect:** Brent traded around $92.65 Thursday, up about 3.5% on the day and roughly 25% over the past month; WTI opened near $84.59 before easing intraday to about $83.80. - **Concept:** Watch the geography, not just the barrel count. Strikes reaching Kuwait and Egypt for the first time don't directly threaten more oil supply than a Jordan strike did — but they widen the set of countries and assets that could plausibly be drawn into direct retaliation, which is what determines whether this stays a contained exchange or becomes something that actually closes a chokepoint. Oil prices this week have moved more on that widening-probability story than on any single physical barrel disrupted.

## 5. Deep Dive — The Fed Holds, the Bond Market Doesn't Believe It: Kevin Warsh's Credibility Problem

**5.1 What happened:** The FOMC voted 9-3 on Wednesday to hold the federal funds rate at 3.50-3.75%, despite the most hawkish pre-meeting setup markets had seen in years — CME FedWatch had priced roughly 38% odds of a hike, a level Bloomberg noted the Fed had never held through without eventually hiking since it began tracking the measure in 2008. Three officials — Cleveland's Beth Hammack, Minneapolis's Neel Kashkari and Dallas's Lorie Logan — dissented in favor of a 25bp hike, the largest same-direction dissent bloc since 2016. In his second press conference since taking the chair in May, Kevin Warsh delivered notably hawkish language ("there is no soft inflation target"; "five-plus years of inflation above target cannot be cured in nine weeks, or by a single month of modest price decreases") while offering no clear signal on the path ahead, consistent with his stated philosophy of stripping back explicit forward guidance. Asked directly why the Fed didn't act given the scale of the dissent, Warsh pointed to the market's own jump in long-end yields as already "doing some of the Fed's work" for it. ([Federal Reserve](https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/pressreleases/monetary20260729a.htm), [CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/07/29/fed-meeting-today-live-updates.html), [Bloomberg](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/features/2026-07-30/how-fed-chief-kevin-warsh-baffled-markets-on-inflation))

**5.2 Why it matters:** Central bank credibility is the mechanism that keeps long-term inflation expectations anchored without the Fed having to move rates dramatically every meeting. When a Fed chair talks tough on inflation but doesn't back it with action, and three sitting officials publicly disagree with the call, the market has to decide for itself whether to trust the "hold" or start pricing in a higher chance that inflation runs hot for longer. Wednesday's price action suggests it chose the latter: the 30-year Treasury yield's jump to its highest level since 2007 is the bond market effectively repricing the inflation-risk premium on its own, rather than waiting for the Fed to confirm one direction or the other. That's a more consequential signal than the 25bp itself, because it reflects a judgment about the institution, not just the number. ([CNN Business](https://www.cnn.com/2026/07/29/business/bond-yields-fed-warsh), [Fortune](https://fortune.com/2026/07/29/kevin-warsh-treasury-yields-stocks-rate-hikes/))

**5.3 What's priced in:** With three officials already on record wanting a hike and Warsh declining to rule one out, Fed-funds futures markets now lean toward a higher probability of a hike at the next meeting than they did going into this one — a reversal of the "unprecedented if it happens" framing that dominated coverage into Wednesday's decision. The bear-steepened curve (2-year down slightly, 30-year up sharply) suggests the market isn't pricing imminent Fed tightening so much as a longer stretch of above-target inflation that the Fed may eventually have to address. ([Yahoo Finance](https://finance.yahoo.com/markets/article/treasury-yields-continue-to-rise-as-wall-street-calls-out-feds-inflation-credibility-shock-122430505.html))

**5.4 Monitor:** Tonight's June PCE print and Q2 GDP advance estimate (8:30pm SGT) — the first hard data since the decision, and the clearest near-term test of whether Warsh's hawkish framing was justified; any inter-meeting speeches from Hammack, Kashkari or Logan pressing their case further; whether long-end yields keep climbing or stabilize once the initial reaction fades; and whether equity markets, which sold off Wednesday, treat tonight's PCE data as confirmation or relief.

## 6. Idea Lab — [[Samsung Electronics]] (KRX: 005930)

*The other end of today's "beat and sold off anyway" story — Samsung just posted the best profit growth in its history and its stock fell regardless, the same pattern that hit its smaller rival SK Hynix twice already this week.*

**6.1 How it makes money:** Samsung Electronics is a diversified conglomerate spanning three main pillars. Device Solutions (memory chips — DRAM and NAND, plus its foundry and System LSI logic-chip business) is the segment driving this week's headlines, and the one most exposed to the AI-capex cycle through high-bandwidth memory (HBM) sales into Nvidia's and other AI-accelerator supply chains. Mobile eXperience (smartphones, tablets, wearables) and Display round out the business, giving Samsung a revenue base far less concentrated in memory than SK Hynix's, which is almost purely a memory-chip pure-play. Three KPIs matter most: (1) HBM qualification progress and shipment volumes, since HBM commands far higher margins than commodity DRAM and is the clearest read on whether Samsung is closing the gap with SK Hynix in AI memory; (2) memory ASPs (average selling prices) across the broader DRAM/NAND cycle, since Samsung's Device Solutions profit swings with the pricing cycle even more than with volume; and (3) foundry utilization and Exynos/System LSI progress, the more capital-intensive, currently loss-making bet on catching up to TSMC in advanced chip manufacturing. ([TradingKey](https://www.tradingkey.com/analysis/stocks/more/262063330-samsung-earnings-beat-fails-lift-kospi-drop-sk-hynix-tumbles-tradingkey))

> [!bullish]
> **6.2 Bull:** Samsung's Q2 operating profit surged 1,813.8% year-on-year to ₩89.49tn, a genuine company record, reflecting a memory-pricing supercycle that Samsung — unlike SK Hynix, which just posted a revenue miss alongside its own record profit — is capturing without the same demand-side warning sign. Its diversification across mobile, display and foundry (versus SK Hynix's pure memory exposure) means a memory-cycle wobble hits Samsung's overall earnings less severely, and any HBM market-share gains against SK Hynix would be additive to an already-record base.

> [!bearish]
> **6.3 Bear:** The stock falling 0.72% despite a 1,813.8% profit beat is the market explicitly telling you the good news was already priced in — the same "priced for perfection" pattern that hit Samsung on Tuesday and SK Hynix twice this week. If SK Hynix's revenue miss and elevated capex guidance really is an early oversupply signal for the memory industry as a whole, Samsung's record quarter doesn't exempt it from that cycle — it may just be one quarter behind. Layered on top: Seoul's new leveraged-ETF curbs (a 20%-of-portfolio cap, effective Friday) directly target the retail leverage that has amplified moves in Samsung and SK Hynix alike, meaning some of this week's price action may be forced deleveraging rather than a fundamentals read at all.

**6.4 Differentiating question:** Is Samsung's muted stock reaction to a record quarter a rational read-through of the same oversupply risk that hit SK Hynix, or is it mechanical spillover from Korea's leveraged-ETF unwind — money being forced out of every large-cap chip name regardless of that name's specific results? The way to find out: watch whether Samsung and SK Hynix stabilize once Friday's accelerated leverage-curb rules actually take effect (1 August), which would point to a market-structure story; if both keep falling on genuinely fresh news (Kioxia's Friday earnings, for instance) even after the forced-selling pressure eases, that points to the oversupply read being the real driver.
