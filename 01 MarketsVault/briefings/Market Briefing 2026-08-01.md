## 2. Theme Tracker

### 2.1 fed-path — US Fed path & inflation — STABLE (holding at an elevated, contested pitch, now with a genuine stagflation data point)
- **What changed:** Fed-funds futures held at roughly 81-82% odds of a September hike Friday, essentially flat versus Thursday's jump from ~52%. What's new is the underlying data: Chicago PMI beat forecasts at 57.6 (up from June's 56.7), a genuinely strong manufacturing signal, but the Q2 Employment Cost Index rose 0.9% quarter-on-quarter (beating the 0.8% consensus, annual pace steady at 3.4%) and — critically — real wage growth (wages minus inflation) turned negative for the first time since Q4 2022. Workers are now losing ground to inflation in real terms even as headline labor-cost growth stays elevated, which is the textbook stagflation tell: growth that isn't translating into real income gains, alongside cost pressure that doesn't go away on its own.
- **Market effect:** US 10-year yield jumped to roughly 4.73-4.75% Friday, its highest level in over a year. DXY held near 99.8-100.4, still soft on the week.
- **Concept:** "Real wage growth" is nominal wage growth minus inflation — it's the number that tells you whether workers can actually buy more with their paychecks, as opposed to just receiving bigger ones. When it goes negative while nominal wage costs are still rising at 3.4% a year, it means inflation is eating the gains faster than employers are granting them — a dynamic that squeezes households (bad for consumption) while doing nothing to cool the wage-price pressure the Fed actually targets. That's why a "beat" on the Employment Cost Index isn't unambiguously good news for the inflation fight.

### 2.2 hormuz-oil — Hormuz / Iran war — STRENGTHENING SHARPLY (the war may be about to enter its most direct phase yet against Iran's own energy infrastructure)
- **What changed:** Everything this week has targeted shipping, shore bases or third-country energy assets caught in the crossfire. What's reportedly being prepared this weekend is different in kind: a direct US-Israeli bombing campaign against Iran's own energy infrastructure, described by officials as potentially one of the harshest of the war, with strikes possible through Saturday and Sunday — and, notably, some discussion of finishing before Monday's market open, which is itself a signal of how seriously officials are weighing the economic blowback. Overnight and Saturday: Kuwait intercepted Iranian drones aimed at a government facility and a Bubiyan Island company site; Iran's FM Araghchi warned Turkey and Pakistan against US "adventurous action" and promised "decisive and proportionate" retaliation; Iran's Fars news agency named Israel's Leviathan and Tamar gas fields plus Saudi, UAE, Qatari, Kuwaiti and Bahraini energy infrastructure as potential retaliation targets; a parliamentary spokesman threatened to "return the entire region... to the Stone Age" if Iran is struck; Israeli assessments (per Channel 12) expect Iranian ballistic missiles regardless of whether the IDF joins any US operation, with Israel on "highest alert" and US refuelling aircraft reportedly massing at Israeli airfields. Separately, Iran formally declared Strait of Hormuz transit "not possible" given ongoing US "aggression," a tanker was hit off Oman by an unidentified projectile (engine-room damage), and the IRGC claims it struck two further transiting vessels. US embassies in Amman, Jerusalem and Baghdad issued Saturday advisories urging citizens to prepare to leave.
- **Market effect:** Brent rose toward $90 and WTI toward $85 Friday as Hormuz traffic "began to falter" again even before the weekend strike reports broke; both are illiquid over the weekend with no fresh print available into Saturday. Exxon and Chevron's already-reported Q2 profits (Exxon $14.53bn, +105% y/y; Chevron $12.07bn, +385% y/y) are the clearest read on how much of the war is already showing up in energy-sector cash flow.
- **Concept:** Watch the difference between strikes on shipping and infrastructure caught in the war's periphery versus a direct campaign against Iran's own energy assets. The former is a transit-risk story that oil-freight and insurance markets have partially adapted to over months (transits already down sharply). The latter would be a supply-side shock to actual production and export capacity — a different, larger order of magnitude for oil prices, and the reason officials are reportedly weighing the timing against Monday's market open rather than just the battlefield calculus.

## 5. Deep Dive — A Weekend Bombing Campaign, Reportedly Timed Around Monday's Open

**5.1 What happened:** US officials told reporters Friday that Washington and Israel are preparing what could be one of the harshest bombing campaigns of the war against Iran's energy infrastructure, with strikes possible throughout Saturday and Sunday. Officials cautioned the operation could still be called off before it starts — but notably, some of the internal discussion has reportedly centered on concluding any campaign before US financial markets open Monday, an explicit acknowledgment that the economic fallout is part of the planning calculus, not an afterthought. On the ground Saturday: Kuwait's military intercepted Iranian drones targeting a government facility and a company site on Bubiyan Island; Iran's Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi warned Turkish and Pakistani officials against US "adventurous action" and promised "decisive and proportionate" retaliation; Iran's Fars news agency named Israel's Leviathan and Tamar gas fields, and energy infrastructure across Saudi Arabia, the UAE, Qatar, Kuwait and Bahrain, as potential retaliation targets; and an Iranian parliamentary spokesman threatened to "return the entire region... to the Stone Age" in the event of an attack. Israeli assessments reported by Channel 12 expect Iranian ballistic-missile retaliation regardless of whether Israel itself joins any US operation, with Israel on "highest alert" and US refuelling aircraft reportedly arriving in-country. Separately, Iran formally declared Strait of Hormuz transit "not possible," a tanker was struck by an unidentified projectile off Oman (engine-room damage reported), and the IRGC claims it struck two more transiting vessels. US embassies in Amman, Jerusalem and Baghdad issued Saturday advisories urging citizens to prepare to leave. ([CBS News](https://www.cbsnews.com/live-updates/iran-war-us-trump-strait-of-hormuz-kuwait-jordan-air-base/), [Times of Israel](https://www.timesofisrael.com/liveblog-august-01-2026/amp/), [Haaretz](https://www.haaretz.com/israel-news/israel-security/2026-08-01/ty-article-live/report-u-s-israel-plan-iran-energy-strikes-as-soon-as-this-weekend/0000019f-bb6a-df0b-a1df-fbee8f4e0004))

**5.2 Why it matters:** Every prior escalation this war has hit shipping, shore bases, or third-country energy assets caught in the crossfire — costly and disruptive, but not a direct assault on Iran's own oil and gas production and export infrastructure. If this weekend's reported campaign proceeds as described, it would be qualitatively different: a supply-side shock aimed at the source, not the transit route. That distinction is the whole ballgame for how oil reacts. The detail that officials are reportedly timing the operation around Monday's market open is itself a data point — it suggests Washington expects a large, negative market reaction if strikes land while markets are open, and would rather manage that reaction than let it happen live.

**5.3 What's priced in:** Almost nothing concrete yet, because markets are closed and the reporting is still "planning, not confirmed." Friday's Brent move toward $90 reflected the ongoing Hormuz-traffic deterioration already in train, not this specific weekend-strike reporting, which broke after Friday's Asia-Pacific close and largely over the US/European weekend news cycle. That means Monday's opening gap in Brent and WTI will be the first genuine market pricing of this specific risk — a much cleaner signal than anything available right now.

**5.4 Monitor:** Whether the strikes are confirmed (by CENTCOM, IDF, or Iranian state media) before Monday's Asia open, or whether — as officials suggested is possible — the operation is called off or delayed; the scale and target list if it proceeds (energy infrastructure specifically, versus a broader mix); Iran's actual retaliation against Israel, the Gulf energy sites Fars named, or Hormuz shipping; and whether Monday's Brent/WTI opening levels suggest the market is pricing a contained event or the start of a materially larger phase of the war.

## 6. Idea Lab — [[Berkshire Hathaway]] (NYSE: BRK.B)

*Rotation to a mega-cap conglomerate/insurer, deliberately away from this week's tech-earnings focus, and timed to Monday's Q2 report — Greg Abel's third quarterly print as CEO since taking over from Warren Buffett on 1 January, with Buffett remaining chairman.*

**6.1 How it makes money:** Berkshire is really three businesses wearing one ticker. First, insurance (GEICO, Berkshire Hathaway Primary Group, Berkshire Hathaway Reinsurance) generates "float" — premiums collected upfront that Berkshire can invest before claims come due — which stood at roughly $176.9bn at Q1's end; Q1 underwriting profit alone rose ~29% y/y to $1.717bn. Second, a portfolio of wholly owned operating businesses (BNSF railroad, Berkshire Hathaway Energy, plus manufacturing/service/retail units) that generate steady cash: BNSF earned $1.38bn and BHE $1.11bn in Q1, both up modestly year-on-year. Third, a large public-equity portfolio and a record cash pile — over $397bn in cash and short-term Treasuries at Q1's end, up roughly $24bn from year-end 2025 — that Buffett (and now Abel) can deploy opportunistically. Three KPIs matter most: (1) **insurance float growth and underwriting margin**, since float is effectively interest-free investment capital as long as underwriting stays profitable; (2) **the cash pile's trajectory** — whether it keeps growing (capital patience, or a lack of attractive opportunities) or starts shrinking (a signal Abel is finding deployment targets); (3) **operating earnings from the wholly owned businesses**, the cleanest read on the non-insurance economy, since it strips out quarter-to-quarter equity-portfolio marks. ([Berkshire Hathaway Q1 2026 release](https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/news/may0226.pdf), [Reinsurance News](https://www.reinsurancene.ws/berkshire-hathaway-reinsurance-underwriting-earnings-rise-29-to-1-717bn-in-q1-2026/))

> [!bullish]
> **6.2 Bull:** A record $397bn-plus cash pile is dry powder precisely when volatility (an active Middle East war, a contested Fed, a violent AI-capex repricing) is creating the kind of dislocated pricing Berkshire has historically bought into; insurance float keeps compounding at a ~29% underwriting-profit growth clip, funding that optionality for free.

> [!bearish]
> **6.3 Bear:** That same cash pile has now sat idle and growing for multiple quarters through one of the most consequential capital-allocation cycles in a generation — the AI buildout — while Abel, six months into the job with no long public track record of his own capital-allocation calls, has yet to show he'll deploy it any faster or differently than an increasingly cautious Buffett already was.

**6.4 Differentiating question:** Does Monday's Q2 report show the cash pile still growing (Berkshire staying defensive through the war/Fed/AI-capex volatility) or does it show Abel making a first real capital-allocation mark of his own — a large buyback, an acquisition, or a meaningful new equity position? The answer says more about how the market should read "Berkshire under Abel" for the next decade than any single quarter's operating numbers.

## 7. Trade Floor — Two Open Tensions

**7.1 FICC/Commodity — Officials say the planned US-Israel strikes on Iran's energy infrastructure could still be called off, and some reports suggest the operation is being timed to finish before Monday's market open. Is that operational planning discipline, or a signal Washington itself expects a market shock too large to manage live?** One side: deliberately sequencing a military operation around a stock market's opening bell is itself evidence officials expect a genuinely disruptive move in oil and broader risk assets — you don't plan around market hours unless you think the market will react hard. Other side: military operations get "called off" constantly for tactical reasons unrelated to markets, and reading a market-timing signal into standard operational caution may be over-interpreting a single detail in still-unconfirmed reporting. *Settling datapoint: whether strikes are confirmed before Monday's Asia open, and the size of Brent's opening gap versus Friday's close near $90.*

**7.2 Equity — The four hyperscalers' combined $720-745bn 2026 capex guide is being read as blanket reassurance on AI-infrastructure demand, even as Amazon (+15%) and Apple (-7-9%) just had opposite weeks on structurally similar spending-and-guidance patterns. Is the market correctly separating "the buildout is real" from "this company's story is credible," or is the aggregate number papering over dispersion that will eventually demand a single, harsher verdict?** One side: markets can and should hold both views — total industry conviction and single-name discrimination aren't contradictory, and rewarding Amazon's trackable AWS backlog while punishing Apple's guidance miss is exactly the kind of discrimination a healthy market should apply. Other side: an aggregate capex number this large, unquestioned, is exactly the kind of comforting headline figure that lets genuine overbuild risk hide in plain sight until a demand air-pocket forces a repricing of the whole group at once, not just the weakest link. *Settling datapoint: Q3 earnings season (Amazon, Microsoft, Alphabet, Meta report roughly late October) and whether AWS growth actually closes the gap with Azure, or the group's aggregate capex-to-revenue conversion starts to diverge company by company.*
