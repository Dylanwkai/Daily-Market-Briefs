## 2. Theme Tracker

### 2.1 fed-path — US Fed path & inflation — VALIDATING (in-line data lets both camps keep their read)
- **What changed:** July CPI printed exactly at consensus — headline 3.4% y/y (unchanged forecast), core 2.5% y/y, the slowest core pace since March 2021. That's a genuinely uneventful print, and the market treated it that way: no violent repositioning, just a continuation of the drift already under way. Post-print, CME's implied probability of a September hike sat around 42-44%, continuing down from 67% two weeks ago (31 July) and 44% a week ago (7 August) — the in-line data didn't reverse the trend, it just failed to accelerate it either way. Fed officials split predictably: Cleveland's Beth Hammack said she lacks "confidence" inflation keeps slowing and still favors hiking; Richmond's Tom Barkin called a hike "still an open question."
- **Market effect:** S&P 500 +0.65% to a record 7,798.99, Nasdaq +0.81% to 26,803.03, Dow +0.13% to 53,839.99. The 10-year Treasury yield eased modestly to roughly 4.68-4.70% from Monday's 4.73% high. VIX extended its decline to the mid-14s.
- **Concept:** When a data print lands exactly at consensus, it doesn't resolve a policy debate — it just fails to move the needle in either direction, so whichever camp was arguing before the print gets to keep arguing after it. That's why the same CPI number produced a hawkish read from Hammack and a genuinely undecided one from Barkin: an in-line print is information-free on the *marginal* question (hike or hold), even though it confirms the broader disinflation trend everyone already agreed on.

### 2.2 hormuz-oil — Hormuz / Iran war — DIVERGING (physical scarcity worsens, but price stops tracking it)
- **What changed:** Shipping transits through the strait fell to just 3 in the 24 hours to Wednesday (2 inbound, 1 outbound, all via the Oman/US-backed southern corridor) — down sharply from 14 the day before, and effectively a full stop for routine commercial traffic. Iran's new security chief, Mohsen Rezaei, restated that the strait stays closed until the US ends its military operations and lifts the blockade. Trump repeated that the US has "total control" and warned Iran could "get blown away," calling the situation "going fine."
- **Market effect:** Despite the traffic collapse, Brent fell roughly 2.1% to around $87 and WTI fell roughly 2.4% to around $81.28 — oil moving the opposite way from the physical-scarcity data, for the first time in this stretch of the story. Gold pulled back slightly off Wednesday's high alongside it (→2.4).
- **Concept:** Official transit counts and actual oil supply aren't the same thing when large operators can route around a chokepoint. Gulf shuttle-tanker fleets — Sinokor, ADNOC, Kuwait's state carriers — are reportedly still moving crude by alternative routes even as the headline "vessels through the strait" number collapses. That gap is why the market didn't reprice on today's traffic data: traders may be pricing the workaround capacity, not just the chokepoint itself. Worth flagging as fragile — if the workaround routes also come under pressure, the price catch-up could be sharp.

### 2.3 ai-capex — AI capex cycle — STRENGTHENING, now with a financing-structure sub-plot
- **What changed:** Two distinct sub-stories moved today. First, Nvidia's $500bn Wall Street financing alliance (full detail in Deep Dive, →5) adds a new layer to the capex story: it's no longer just about hyperscalers spending, but about who underwrites the spending and how. Second, the memory-chip supply chain kept confirming itself in real time — Applied Materials beat and raised on record equipment demand (→6), Kospi rallied a fourth straight session, and Samsung/SK Hynix's rumored shareholder-return plan firmed up to a reported "above 200 trillion won" scale. Overnight AI-infrastructure earnings were a genuine mixed bag: Cisco beat and raised (FY27 Q1 guide well above Street) but fell after-hours anyway; Coherent beat but saw conflicting stock reaction; Cerebras' cloud revenue nearly quadrupled but a $450.5m GAAP net loss sent shares down 18% after-hours; Nebius surged 34% on a smaller beat.
- **Market effect:** Nasdaq +0.81% Wednesday on the CPI-plus-earnings combination; Kospi +3.56% to 6,813.34 on the chip-supply-chain read; Cerebras -18% after-hours as the one clear loser in the earnings batch, a reminder that "AI capex is booming" doesn't mean every AI-linked earnings print clears the bar.
- **Concept:** A capex boom this large eventually raises a second-order question beyond "how much is being spent" — namely, "on whose balance sheet, and who bears the loss if demand disappoints." Nvidia arranging financing for its own customers, and Meta/Oracle/xAI routing tens of billions through off-balance-sheet vehicles, both push AI spending further from the direct balance sheets of the companies actually buying the chips. That can make the spending easier to fund short-term, but it also means the credit risk becomes harder to see from the outside — which is exactly the BIS's complaint (→5).

### 2.4 bond-regime — Bond market regime — CALM EXTENDS post-print
- **What changed:** The 10-year yield eased to ~4.68-4.70% from Monday's ~4.73% peak, a muted move consistent with an in-line print. VIX extended its decline into the mid-14s. Gold pulled back slightly off Wednesday's high to the low-$4,400s, and Bitcoin held roughly flat around $63,400-63,560 — still not participating in gold's move.
- **Market effect:** Every major vol/hedge gauge moved in the same "risk-off pressure easing" direction at once — yields down, VIX down, gold pausing its climb — a coherent signal, unlike Tuesday's slightly mixed one.
- **Concept:** Gold pausing its rally right as VIX falls further is a meaningful pairing: gold's bid has mostly been about slow-moving inflation-erosion worry, not near-term market-crash fear, so an in-line CPI print that removes one specific near-term catalyst (a hot surprise) can cool gold's bid a little even without resolving the longer-run inflation debate Hammack is still fighting for (→2.1).

## 5. Deep Dive — Nvidia's $500 Billion Wall Street Financing Alliance

**5.1 What happened:** Nvidia signed memorandums of understanding this week (10-11 August) with six of the largest pools of capital on Wall Street — Apollo, BlackRock, Blackstone, Brookfield, Goldman Sachs and KKR — to mobilize $500bn or more in third-party financing for AI data-center construction, with the first deals expected to reach market within months. CEO Jensen Huang described the shift as treating AI compute as "an investable asset class" in its own right, moving away from project-by-project chip purchases toward "AI factories" financed the way toll roads or power plants are. The announcement landed within days of the Bank for International Settlements' 2026 Annual Report naming an AI-capex bust, the collapse of "circular financing" arrangements, and sovereign-debt fragility as its top three risks to global financial stability, and Senator Elizabeth Warren pressing the Financial Stability Oversight Council to formally investigate more than $1 trillion of AI-linked debt, much of it in opaque private-credit structures. ([Bloomberg](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-10/nvidia-to-team-with-wall-street-on-500-billion-package-ft-says), [CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/08/10/nvidia-wall-street-asset-managers-500-billion-ai-push.html), [Seeking Alpha — BIS](https://seekingalpha.com/article/4920057-bis-warning-ai-bubble-burst-credit-event-inflation-fiscal-problems))

**5.2 Why it matters:** This is a different layer of the AI-capex story than anything covered so far this week. Alphabet's $25bn bond sale (covered in Tuesday's edition) was one company borrowing against its own balance sheet. This is Nvidia — the chip supplier itself — actively arranging financing for the customers who buy its chips, which blurs the line between "vendor" and "lender" in a way regulators explicitly flagged as a systemic risk just days earlier. It also sits alongside a broader pattern: Oracle, Meta, xAI and CoreWeave have already moved more than $120bn of AI data-center spending off their own balance sheets into special-purpose vehicles (SPVs) — legal entities set up specifically to hold an asset and its financing separately from the parent company's books — including Meta's $30bn "Hyperion" vehicle backed by Pimco, BlackRock, Apollo and Blue Owl. ([Bloomberg](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-11/nvidia-s-show-of-financial-force-soothes-jittery-credit-markets), [Cryptopolitan](https://www.cryptopolitan.com/oracle-xai-meta-coreweave-off%E2%80%91balance-sheet/))

**5.3 What's priced in / what to monitor:** The initial market read was reassurance, not alarm — Bloomberg's own framing was that the deal "soothed jittery credit markets," and it arguably does reduce near-term funding-availability risk for AI buildouts. What it doesn't resolve is the BIS's actual complaint: that circular financing arrangements make it harder to see where the credit risk really sits if AI demand disappoints. Three things to watch: the terms and credit spread on the first executed Nvidia-backed compute-financing deal (expected within months); whether spreads on AI-linked private-credit and high-yield paper widen or hold as more of this financing structure becomes visible; and what color, if any, Nvidia's own Q2 FY27 earnings on 26 August (→8.1) give on how much of this financing has actually converted to signed deals.

## 6. Idea Lab — Applied Materials (NASDAQ: AMAT)

Today's news: Applied Materials reported fiscal Q3 2026 results after Thursday's US close, landing in the same week the memory-chip supply chain it sells equipment into is rallying on the Samsung/SK Hynix shareholder-return story (→2.3, 1.3) — a look at the AI-capex trade from the equipment layer rather than the chip layer.

**(a) How it actually makes money:** [[Applied Materials]] sells the manufacturing tools chipmakers use to build semiconductors, split into two segments: Semiconductor Systems (roughly 75% of revenue — within that, foundry/logic tools are about 67%, DRAM about 29%, NAND about 4%) and Applied Global Services (AGS), the recurring, higher-margin business of servicing and upgrading the installed base of tools already in factories. Q3 revenue came in at $9.12bn (+24.8% y/y, just shy of the $9.18bn Street estimate), adjusted EPS of $3.50 beat the $3.45 estimate — a fifth straight beat — and the Q4 guide of $10.25bn revenue / $3.82-4.22 EPS came in well above consensus. Semiconductor Systems operating margin expanded to 38.0% as orders accelerated across DRAM, leading-edge logic and advanced packaging. The KPIs that matter: (1) China revenue mix — it fell to 28% of total from 35% the prior quarter, the cleanest available read on how much US export controls are actually biting; (2) DRAM/HBM4 and gate-all-around (GAA, the newest leading-edge transistor architecture) order intake, tied to Applied's "EPIC Center" partnerships with Samsung, SK Hynix and Micron; (3) AGS service-revenue growth, which matters because it's the part of the business that keeps growing even if new-tool orders go through a cyclical air pocket. ([TradingKey](https://www.tradingkey.com/analysis/stocks/us-stocks/262098841-applied-materials-amat-q3-earnings-august-13-2026-semiconductor-equipment-ai-tradingkey), [24/7 Wall St.](https://247wallst.com/cards/applied-materials-q3-2026-earnings-amat-01kzybvthd5qqamwrtqk59k25x))

> [!bullish]
> **Bull in one line:** Structural AI/HBM4/GAA/advanced-packaging capex — hyperscaler spending tracking above $600bn in 2026 and projected above $700bn in 2027 — locks in multi-year tool demand, and Applied's EPIC Center partnerships with all three major memory makers put it at the center of that build.

> [!bearish]
> **Bear in one line:** China was still 28% of revenue even after a step-down from 35%, export controls keep tightening (Applied already paid a $252.5m settlement over violations tied to SMIC), and a 32x-forward-earnings multiple leaves little room for either a China shock or a cyclical pause in tool orders.

**(c) One question for a differentiated view:** Is this quarter's beat-and-raise driven by durable, multi-year HBM4 and GAA capacity building, or is some of it China customers pulling orders forward ahead of the next round of export-control tightening? The October-quarter book-to-bill ratio (the ratio of new orders to units shipped/billed — a forward-looking demand signal) and the next Bureau of Industry and Security licensing action on China would start to answer that. Learning tool, not a recommendation. ([Manufacturing Dive](https://www.manufacturingdive.com/news/applied-materials-252-2m-penalty-bis-smic-china-export-violations/812159/))

**7.2 (FICC/FX) With the Bank of Japan's policy rate near 0.75% and the 10-year JGB yield at roughly 2.015% — its highest since 1999 — and Morgan Stanley estimating around $500bn of yen "carry trade" positions still outstanding, does further BOJ tightening trigger a disorderly unwind of those positions, or has the market already absorbed the risk?** A carry trade means borrowing cheaply in one currency (yen, historically near-zero rates) to fund higher-yielding assets elsewhere; when the funding currency's rates rise, those positions can unwind fast, as they did violently in August 2024. One side: JGB yields at a 26-year high mean the "cheap funding" premise of the yen carry trade keeps eroding, and any BOJ surprise could force a fast, correlated unwind across multiple asset classes at once, just as it did two years ago. Other side: an emerging-market carry index is down only about 1% this time versus roughly 4% during the 2024 episode, suggesting positions have already been trimmed and prior US-Japan currency coordination may have defused the tail risk. *Settling datapoint: USD/JPY volatility and positioning data around the next scheduled BOJ policy meeting.* ([Japan Times](https://www.japantimes.co.jp/business/2026/08/06/markets/emerging-market-yen-carry-trade/), [Investing.com](https://www.investing.com/analysis/the-boj-just-pulled-the-trigger-markets-brace-for-carry-trade-chaos-200672097))

Nvidia arranging $500bn of third-party financing for the same customers buying its chips — days after the BIS flagged "circular financing" in AI debt as a top systemic risk — is the sharpest thing to sit with today. Question worth forming a view on: does formalizing this financing with regulated, well-capitalized names like BlackRock and Goldman actually reduce the systemic risk the BIS is worried about, by bringing an opaque private-credit story into more transparent structures — or does it just make the circularity bigger and harder to unwind precisely because it now runs through the balance sheets everyone assumes are safe? Watch the credit spread on Nvidia's first executed compute-financing deal for an early answer.
