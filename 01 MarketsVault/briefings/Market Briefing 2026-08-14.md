## 2. Theme Tracker

### 2.1 fed-path — US Fed path & inflation — TWO-SIDED (soft headline, hot core, weak consumer)
- **What changed:** July PPI landed flat m/m (vs. +0.2% expected) and +4.7% y/y (down from June's 5.5%) — a soft headline. But core services inflation excluding trade margins jumped to +0.6% m/m from +0.2% in June, a genuinely hot undercurrent. CME's implied odds of a September hike fell from the low-40s to roughly 32-35%. Cleveland's Beth Hammack pushed back the same day: "I love to see that those numbers are coming in lower... but I don't have confidence that we're going to continue to see that." Then Friday's own data cut the other way on growth: July retail sales fell 0.6% m/m (the sharpest drop in more than a year, led by autos -1.8% and online sales -2.2%), and preliminary August University of Michigan consumer sentiment sank to 51.0 from July's final 55.2, well below the 54.5 consensus.
- **Market effect:** S&P 500, Nasdaq and Dow all held near Thursday's record levels on the soft PPI (→3.1). The Treasury's 30-year bond auction priced at 5.216% — the highest yield for that tenor since 2001 — even as the 10-year eased modestly to ~4.65-4.68%, a sign the long end is pricing something the front end isn't. VIX stayed pinned near 14.6, a seven-month low.
- **Concept:** PPI measures what producers charge each other, before it reaches consumers — economists watch it as a leading indicator for where CPI is headed next, since a cost increase at the wholesale level usually shows up at the register with a lag. A soft headline PPI with hot core services is the trickiest combination to read: it says goods-price disinflation (autos, energy, construction inputs) is masking sticky services inflation (the labor-heavy stuff — insurance, medical care, portfolio management fees) that the Fed's rate tool has less direct leverage over. That's why Hammack can look at the same print everyone's calling "cooling" and still not budge.

### 2.2 hormuz-oil — Hormuz / Iran war — ESCALATING (fresh attacks, price starts catching up)
- **What changed:** Houthi drones/missiles hit Saudi Aramco's Jazan refinery (400,000 bpd capacity) on 9 August, forcing a shutdown Aramco says won't be resolved until later this month. Then on 13 August, two ADNOC (UAE) vessels came under attack transiting the Strait — the second such incident on ADNOC ships in under a week — prompting a formal UAE protest citing violation of UN Security Council Resolution 2817 on freedom of navigation. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent told Newsmax the US is preparing measures to "economically isolate" Iran unlike anything seen before, and the US naval blockade of Iranian ports could continue "indefinitely." Iran and Oman have still not finalized the managed-transit deal Tehran said it was "very close" to on 8 August.
- **Market effect:** Oil, which had actually been falling through Wednesday and Thursday even as Hormuz transits collapsed (a divergence flagged in Wednesday's edition), reversed higher Friday morning — Brent +1.7% to $88.52, WTI +1.4% to $82.40 — as the fresh, visible attacks forced a repricing that the abstract "transit count" data hadn't. Gulf sovereign risk is a live question again too: full detail in Global Pulse (6b).
- **Concept:** Markets tend to underprice slow-building, already-known risks and overreact to a fresh, concrete headline — even when the headline doesn't actually change the underlying picture much. Three vessel transits a day versus fourteen is abstract; a named tanker getting hit is not. That's why oil moved more on two attacked ships Thursday night than it did on a week of collapsing transit data — the market was pricing the story it could see, not the one the data was already telling it.

### 2.3 ai-capex — AI capex cycle — BIFURCATING (hardware-layer margins squeezed by the boom's own inputs)
- **What changed:** Applied Materials beat and raised — record Q3 revenue of $9.12bn (+25% y/y), a fifth straight EPS beat, Q4 guide well above Street — yet fell roughly 2% in Thursday's session and another ~5% after-hours, a "sell the news" reaction after its 100%+ year-to-date run. Cisco reported record Q4 revenue ($17.25bn, +18% y/y) and record AI-infrastructure orders ($9.3bn for FY26), yet fell 8-9% as gross margin compressed to 66.3% from 68.4%, driven by rising costs for the memory chips that go into its own AI networking gear. Cerebras missed both revenue ($180.1m vs. $194m expected) and gross margin (14% vs. 26.3% expected) despite raising full-year guidance. Separately, SK Hynix took a controlling stake in Kioxia, consolidating more of the memory supply chain under fewer hands, and Lambda Labs closed a $926m investment-grade-rated term loan — the first IG-rated debt from a "neocloud" AI infrastructure provider. Samsung and SK Hynix's rumored 200-trillion-won-plus shareholder-return plan is still not formalized; Samsung's CFO says the board is still "in deep discussions," with a decision possible by end-August.
- **Market effect:** Kospi paused Thursday exactly at Wednesday's 6,813.34 close, then broke out again Friday, +2.41% to 6,977.34 — a fifth up day in six — after SanDisk's own decision to return excess cash to shareholders gave Samsung (+2.4-3%) and SK Hynix (+3.3-5.8%) a fresh capital-return read-through. Nikkei extended to a fourth straight gain, +0.59% to 68,714, its best week in roughly two months.
- **Concept:** A capex boom this large has started eating its own inputs. Cisco and other hardware makers sell equipment *into* the AI buildout, but they also have to *buy* memory chips to build that equipment — and memory is itself in an AI-driven shortage. So the same boom that's filling their order books is simultaneously raising their input costs, which is exactly why a company can report record orders and still miss on margin. It's a useful reminder that "AI capex is booming" and "every AI-linked stock benefits equally" are not the same statement — the winners are increasingly determined by where a company sits in the supply chain, not just whether it's exposed to the theme at all.

### 2.4 bond-regime — Bond market regime — CALM ON THE SURFACE, TENSION IN THE DETAIL
- **What changed:** The 10-year Treasury yield eased modestly to ~4.65-4.68% on the soft PPI, but Thursday's 30-year auction priced at 5.216%, the highest for that tenor since 2001 — the front end is easing on lower near-term hike odds while the long end is getting squeezed by issuance and term-premium pressure, a genuine curve tension. VIX stayed pinned at 14.63, a seven-month low, even as gold logged its best week since January (+7.3% to ~$4,390) — and gold's own implied volatility (GVZ) rose 3.1% to 25.64, about 1.7x the VIX level. Bitcoin kept drifting the other way, down to roughly $62,700-62,800 from ~$63,400 a day earlier.
- **Market effect:** Equities calm and near records; gold rallying hard on its own track; Bitcoin softening; a full unpacking of the gold/VIX divergence and its likely driver is in Global Pulse (6c).
- **Concept:** Different volatility indices price different specific fears, not just "how nervous is everyone in general." VIX tracks S&P 500 options and mostly reflects near-term macro/policy/earnings risk to US equities. GVZ tracks gold options and reflects a narrower set of worries — currency debasement, central-bank buying dynamics, safe-haven demand. When VIX sits at cycle lows and GVZ rises at the same time, it means the options market isn't broadly scared — it's specifically hedging something about gold, which this week looks like the weak-labor-data-driven dovish repricing plus continued official-sector gold buying, not a general risk-off mood (→6c).

## 5. Deep Dive — Silver Lake's Bid for Workday, and What It Says About the "SaaSpocalypse"

**5.1 What happened:** Reuters reported exclusively on 13 August that private-equity firm Silver Lake has been in talks "for several months" to take Workday — the cloud HR and finance software maker — private, at a valuation around Workday's pre-news market cap of roughly $43bn. The stock surged as much as 26% intraday, was halted for trading multiple times, and closed up 17-18% at $206.45, pushing its market value to roughly $51bn. Nothing is confirmed — talks are ongoing and could still collapse — and Silver Lake could bring in co-investors, as it did on its ~$55bn Electronic Arts take-private earlier this year. Notably, founder Aneel Bhusri returned as CEO only in February 2026, shortly before these talks reportedly began. ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/08/13/workday-skyrockets-25percent-before-trading-halted-on-report-of-silver-lake-takeover.html), [BNN Bloomberg](https://www.bnnbloomberg.ca/business/company-news/2026/08/13/silver-lake-in-talks-to-buy-workday-sources-say-reuters-exclusive/))

**5.2 Why it matters:** This is potentially one of the largest software buyouts in history, and it lands squarely inside a broader 2026 narrative sometimes called the "SaaSpocalypse" — the fear that AI agents will increasingly replicate what licensed, seat-based enterprise software does, cannibalizing the subscription-revenue model that built companies like Workday. Workday itself has been a poster child for that fear: down roughly 15% year-to-date and more than 40% off its 2024 peak heading into this news, even as its actual subscription revenue kept growing (+14.5% in its last fiscal year) — just more slowly than before (growth has decelerated from roughly 16% to 13%). A confirmed deal would be read as a strong signal that private capital sees real value in battered SaaS incumbents that public markets have already marked down for a threat that hasn't fully materialized yet — the read-through lifted Salesforce and ServiceNow on the news too. ([Investing.com sector read-through](https://uk.investing.com/news/stock-market-news/silver-lake-in-talks-to-acquire-workday-what-a-50b-deal-means-for-saas-stocks-93CH-4833263))

**5.3 What's priced in / what to monitor:** The market has already priced in a meaningful takeover premium — the stock trades around $206 versus its pre-news level near $175. Three things to watch: (1) confirmation or denial from either party, and whether Silver Lake brings in co-investors the way it did for EA; (2) read-through trading in Salesforce, ServiceNow and HubSpot if the deal is confirmed or collapses; (3) whether other beaten-down SaaS names start trading with visible takeover speculation baked in, which would be the clearest sign the market has decided this is a sector-wide repricing rather than a one-off.
