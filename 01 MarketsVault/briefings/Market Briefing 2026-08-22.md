## 2. Theme Tracker

### 2.1 fed-path — US Fed path & inflation — STABLE, all eyes on Warsh's Friday debut
- **What changed:** Little Fed-specific news since Thursday's hawkish-leaning FOMC-minutes reaction. What's sharpened over the week is the tension between market consensus and the committee's actual internal split: 69% of fund managers now expect Warsh's Jackson Hole keynote (Friday 28 August, 10am ET) to strike a neutral tone, yet July's meeting produced the first three-way same-direction dissent since 2016 — three regional presidents voting for a hike the committee didn't deliver. Warsh has told reporters his remarks will focus on "long-term structural questions," explicitly downplaying near-term guidance, which markets are reading as an attempt to lower the bar for what the speech needs to deliver.
- **Market effect:** Muted and indirect again this week — Friday's yield and equity moves were driven by the Treasury buyback story and oil/Iran headlines, not fresh Fed repricing. September hike-odds trackers remain choppy in the low-to-mid 30s.
- **Concept:** When a market has a strong consensus view about an upcoming event ("Warsh will be neutral"), the actual risk isn't that the event goes as expected — it's that a small deviation from consensus moves markets disproportionately, because so little of that scenario is priced as a tail case. A "neutral" speech from a chair guiding down expectations, delivered to a committee where three voters already wanted to hike, has more room to surprise hawkish than dovish — worth remembering heading into a week where Jackson Hole will dominate every other headline.

### 2.2 hormuz-oil — Hormuz / Iran war — TURNING, genuinely two-sided for the first time in months
- **What changed:** After weeks of one-directional escalation (culminating in last Friday's frozen talks and Oman-bombing threat), this week produced Iran's most conciliatory public statement of the war: President Pezeshkian said Friday "it is better that we bring the war to an end now as we are in a position of power and dignity," and defended June's memorandum of understanding against domestic critics who call it capitulation, saying "they cannot find even a single clause in this agreement that indicates capitulation — all the commitments concern the other side." Iran's armed forces chief simultaneously warned of "crushing, punishing and devastating responses" to any fresh threats, underlining that conciliatory diplomacy and military posturing are running in parallel, not sequentially. Meanwhile Washington hasn't softened at all: Treasury Secretary Bessent confirmed he'll detail the promised "toughest sanctions in history" at a press conference Monday 24 August (2pm ET), reiterating his goal is to "collapse" the Iranian regime and warning other countries "you're either with us or against us." China's foreign ministry again rejected the sanctions push Friday, calling for a "political and diplomatic approach" instead — setting up Monday's announcement as a real test of whether Washington actually sanctions Chinese buyers of Iranian crude.
- **Market effect:** Brent closed Friday at $94.39, a second straight ~6% weekly gain, still near the four-week highs flagged last Friday. Notably, oil's continued strength this week has come even as physical transit data suggests Hormuz flows have partly normalized via the US-Navy-escorted Omani corridor (→ Deep Dive, 5) — a genuine disconnect between the price action and the on-the-ground supply picture worth digging into.
- **Concept:** A war can de-escalate militarily and diplomatically at the same time that a government maintains hawkish rhetoric for domestic political reasons — Pezeshkian's speech was delivered partly to answer parliamentary critics accusing him of capitulating, which is a different audience than the message being sent to Washington. Markets tend to read government statements literally, but distinguishing a statement's intended domestic audience from its intended international audience is often the difference between correctly reading a genuine policy shift and over-reacting to political theater.

### 2.3 ai-capex — AI capex cycle — STRENGTHENING in demand, with the financing-risk overlay now showing up in hard credit numbers
- **What changed:** The financing-risk thread this tracker has followed since mid-August (Broadcom's XPV downgrade, Nvidia's $500bn financing alliance) now has explicit market pricing behind it: Oracle's 5-year CDS spread has climbed to roughly 203bp — the highest level since the 2008 financial crisis, up from 144bp at the start of 2026 — while Nvidia's own CDS sits in the high-60s to high-70s bp, both well above the broader investment-grade index (~53bp). Total net notional CDS outstanding on the handful of AI-linked mega-cap names has hit a record $12.5bn, roughly 5x its level from Q2 2025, with Oracle alone accounting for $6.5bn of that. This is happening against a backdrop of continued spending acceleration, not slowdown: combined 2026 capex across Alphabet, Amazon, Meta, Microsoft and Oracle is tracking above $690bn (+80%+ y/y), funded partly by a record $182bn of investment-grade bond issuance from Big Tech so far this year, with free cash flow for several of these companies approaching zero or turning negative as spending front-loads. Elsewhere, Samsung's ₩15tn buyback tranche formally begins Monday 24 August, the first live test of last week's headline $80bn shareholder-return plan.
- **Market effect:** Nvidia closed Friday at $215.38 (+0.31%), roughly flat into next Wednesday's earnings; consensus sits at $93-95bn revenue. Credit markets, not equities, are where this week's AI-financing anxiety is showing up most visibly.
- **Concept:** Credit default swaps price the probability of default over a fixed horizon, independent of whether a company's stock or revenue is growing — which is exactly why CDS spreads can widen sharply even while a company is executing well operationally. Oracle's spread hitting a 2008-crisis-era level doesn't mean the market thinks Oracle is likely to default; it means the market is now demanding meaningfully more compensation for that tail risk than it was six months ago, specifically because so much of the AI buildout is being funded with debt against revenue that's still largely contracted-but-not-yet-delivered. That's a genuinely different risk signal than an equity multiple compressing, and it tends to show up in credit spreads before it shows up in a stock price — one reason this tracker has kept flagging it as a distinct thread from the AI-capex demand story itself.

### 2.4 bond-regime — Bond market regime — WEAKENING, the buyback story has now failed for a full week
- **What changed:** Friday's session confirmed what Thursday's round-trip suggested: the Treasury's expanded buyback program produced one day of relief (Wednesday) before yields not only reversed but extended higher — the 10-year closed near 4.73% and the 30-year near 5.27%, both above where they stood before Bessent's announcement. Unlike last Friday, when equities absorbed the yield move on strong PMI data, this week the S&P 500, Dow and Nasdaq all posted weekly losses (-1.4%, -0.8%, -2%) despite Friday's individual-session gains — the "decoupling" flagged as fragile in last week's tracker didn't hold across the full week.
- **Market effect:** US 10-year ~4.73% (+4bp Fri), 30-year ~5.27% (+3bp); S&P 500 -1.4% week, Nasdaq -2% week, Dow -0.8% week; gold near $4,600-4,620 (fresh high) and bitcoin +22% week — both classic debasement hedges rallying hard exactly as long yields push higher on fiscal-credibility concerns rather than growth strength.
- **Concept:** A "term premium" is the extra yield investors demand to hold longer-dated bonds beyond what's justified by expected short-term rates alone — compensation for the extra risk and uncertainty of lending for longer. When term premium rises because of fiscal-credibility concerns (more debt issuance, doubts about the government's long-run ability or willingness to keep debt sustainable) rather than because growth or inflation expectations are rising, it's a genuinely bad kind of yield increase: it pressures equity valuations without the offsetting benefit of a stronger growth outlook, and it's exactly the environment where "hard" assets like gold and, increasingly, bitcoin tend to outperform financial assets — which is precisely this week's pattern.

## 5. Deep Dive — The Strait Is Quietly Reopening, Even as Washington Threatens to Bomb the Mediator

**5.1 What happened:** Beneath this week's headline escalation — Trump's threat to bomb Oman, Bessent's promised "economic D-Day" — the physical picture in the Strait of Hormuz has been moving in the opposite direction. According to reporting this week, more than 80% of liquids transiting the Strait over the past two weeks have used the Omani route, a UN-authorized shipping channel that Iran officially opposes but that the US Navy has been actively escorting tankers through. US Energy Secretary Chris Wright said the Navy has helped move roughly 15 million barrels of oil and products a day through the strait via this corridor, plus another 5 million barrels via pipeline routes that bypass Hormuz entirely — with 15-20 tankers reportedly transiting nightly. That's a sharp reversal from the picture described in this tracker's 15 August edition, when just two vessels crossed the Strait in a single day and no crude cargoes were visible transiting at all. Separately, a lower estimate cited elsewhere this week put daily volume moving via "covert tanker convoys" at 5-10 million barrels — the range varies by source, but the direction is consistent: meaningfully more oil is moving than two weeks ago, not less. ([Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/8/22/iranian-president-says-time-to-end-war-with-us-from-position-of))

**5.2 Why it matters:** This complicates the simple "war escalating, supply collapsing" narrative that dominated last Friday's briefing. Two things are true simultaneously: Washington's rhetoric toward Iran (and now toward Oman itself) has become more aggressive, not less, while the actual physical flow of oil through the region's most important chokepoint has partially normalized under direct US Navy protection. That's a meaningfully different risk picture than a genuine supply collapse — it suggests Washington's strategy has shifted from trying to negotiate transit rights through diplomacy (the Oman-brokered deal flagged as the likely off-ramp last week) to simply forcing transit through military escort, which sidesteps the need for Iran's cooperation entirely. It also helps explain a genuine puzzle in this week's price action: Brent is still up roughly 6% on the week and near four-week highs, even though the physical scarcity story that justified oil's spike two weeks ago has partly reversed. That gap between the price and the physical picture is either evidence markets haven't caught up to the Navy-escort data yet, or evidence that oil's premium is now pricing sanctions and escalation risk rather than actual current scarcity.

**5.3 What's priced in / what to monitor:** If Navy-escorted transit is genuinely running near 80% of pre-crisis-adjusted volumes, oil's continued ~6% weekly gains look increasingly like a risk premium for what happens next (Monday's sanctions announcement, whether Oman's diplomatic track survives the bombing threat) rather than a reflection of current physical tightness — which means the mispricing risk, if any, currently runs toward oil being too high rather than too low. Three things worth watching: (1) whether Monday's Bessent announcement targets the shipping/insurance infrastructure that's enabling the Navy-escorted route itself, which could reverse this week's flow recovery; (2) whether Iran responds to the Omani-route workaround with fresh attacks specifically targeting escorted convoys, testing whether military escort is actually sustainable at scale; (3) whether Pezeshkian's conciliatory domestic messaging (→2.2) translates into any signal that Tehran will stop contesting the Omani route's legitimacy, which would be the clearest tell that a genuine de-escalation is underway rather than parallel-tracked rhetoric.

## 6. Idea Lab — [[Strategy]] (Nasdaq: MSTR)

*Rotation to a name that directly monetizes this week's dominant cross-asset story — the bitcoin/gold "debasement trade" (→1.3, 2.4) — through a business model built entirely around it, a useful contrast with Idea Lab picks on the fundamentals side of recent weeks.*

**(a) How it actually makes money:** Strategy (formerly MicroStrategy) is really two businesses bolted together: a small, slow-growing enterprise analytics software unit, and a leveraged corporate bitcoin treasury that now dominates the company's identity and valuation. It holds roughly 845,000 bitcoin — about 4% of the 21 million that will ever exist — making it the largest corporate bitcoin holder globally. It funds bitcoin purchases mainly through convertible notes and preferred-stock issuance rather than operating cash flow, and its own headline KPI, "BTC Yield," measures growth in bitcoin-per-fully-diluted-share over time — a way of showing whether its capital raises are adding bitcoin exposure per share (accretive) or diluting it (destructive). The single most important number determining whether that model works is **mNAV** (modified net asset value): the ratio of MSTR's market capitalization to the market value of its bitcoin holdings. When mNAV sits above 1x, Strategy can issue new shares or converts and use the proceeds to buy more bitcoin than the new shares represent on a per-share basis — genuinely accretive. When mNAV falls below 1x, the same math runs in reverse, and every capital raise dilutes existing holders' bitcoin-per-share. MSTR's mNAV fell to roughly 0.68x by 3 August 2026 — a steep discount that effectively froze its ability to raise accretive capital — before this week's 22% bitcoin surge helped push shares up 6-7% Friday to roughly $112 (from a $90-105 range it had been stuck in for six weeks) and mNAV back toward roughly parity, estimates ranging 0.98x-1.05x depending on the source and day. The third KPI that matters: the maturity schedule and coupon structure of its convertible debt and preferred stock, since a sustained bitcoin drawdown large enough to threaten those obligations would force a genuinely difficult choice between diluting shareholders and selling bitcoin. ([TradingKey](https://www.tradingkey.com/news/market-movers/262125090-market-movers-mstr-20260821), [mNAV.com](https://www.mnav.com/mnav/strategy), [SatsIntel](https://www.satsintel.io/en/treasuries/microstrategy))

> [!bullish]
> **Bull in one line:** As the largest corporate bitcoin holder, Strategy is the most direct leveraged proxy for the debasement trade now visibly driving both bitcoin and gold, and this week's mNAV recovery toward parity — from a 0.68x discount just three weeks ago — reopens the accretive-capital-raise flywheel that the whole model depends on.

> [!bearish]
> **Bear in one line:** The entire structure only works while mNAV stays above 1x, and a multi-week stretch trading at a steep discount just weeks ago shows how quickly that flywheel can jam — a renewed bitcoin drawdown would leave Strategy dependent on dilutive raises or selling bitcoin to service converts and preferred obligations, the opposite of the model's premise.

**(c) One question for a differentiated view:** Is this week's mNAV recovery evidence that the market's structural willingness to pay a premium for indirect, liquid, leveraged bitcoin exposure is reasserting itself as bitcoin momentum returns — or was the multi-week discount below 1x in early August a more durable repricing (institutional investors increasingly preferring direct spot-ETF exposure over a leveraged corporate wrapper, especially after this year's ETF-inflow data) that this week's rally has only temporarily masked? The next stretch of sideways-to-down bitcoin price action, and whether mNAV holds near parity or slips back toward a discount, is the clearest test. Learning tool, not a recommendation.
