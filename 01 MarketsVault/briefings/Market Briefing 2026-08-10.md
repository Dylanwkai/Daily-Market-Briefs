## 2. Theme Tracker

### 2.1 fed-path — US Fed path & inflation — TURNING (oil-driven inflation risk is clawing back Friday's dovish repricing)
- **What changed:** No new on-the-record Fed commentary since Sunday — the move is being driven by the oil market, not a Fed speaker. September rate-hike odds bounced to roughly 48-54% on Kalshi and Polymarket Monday, up from Friday's post-payrolls trough of 33-44%, though still well below the ~56-61% peak hit last week when Chair Warsh privately signalled he'd back a hike on a hot inflation print. The mechanism: Sunday's Hormuz-deal doubt (→2.2) revived the same energy-inflation risk that drove three regional Fed presidents to dissent hawkish at the July 29 meeting, partially undoing Friday's "weak jobs report kills hike odds" move.
- **Market effect:** US 10-year yield rose more than 4bp intraday to ~4.70% before easing back toward 4.65-4.66%, reversing part of Friday's ~7bp drop. DXY firmed marginally to ~99.65-99.80 (from Friday's 99.56 close). US equity futures are little changed (S&P +0.07%, Nasdaq +0.27%, Dow -0.11% premarket) — the oil-inflation story hasn't yet translated into a real risk-off move in stocks.
- **Concept:** Rate-hike odds aren't just a Fed-speaker output — they're a running bet on what inflation will look like by the next meeting, and oil is one of the fastest-moving inputs into that bet. A 5% jump in Brent doesn't change today's CPI reading, but it changes the market's forecast of *next* month's reading, because energy costs feed into headline inflation with almost no lag. That's why hike odds can swing on a Sunday oil headline with zero new Fed commentary: the market is repricing the data the Fed will see, not the Fed's reaction function itself. Wednesday's actual CPI print is what tells us whether that oil-driven fear was justified.

### 2.2 hormuz-oil — Hormuz / Iran war — STRENGTHENING (Iran puts a price on reopening, and it's steep)
- **What changed:** Araghchi confirmed Sunday that the Iran-Oman interim transit-corridor deal is in its "final stages" — but for the first time laid out, in concrete terms, what Iran actually wants before the Strait itself reopens: a full US withdrawal of its naval blockade, war reparations, unfreezing of Iranian assets, broad sanctions relief, and a *permanent* end to the conflict against Iran and its regional allies, Yemen's Houthis included. That's a materially harder set of terms than last week's vaguer "very close to a deal" framing, and it explicitly decouples the (nearly done) corridor arrangement from any real de-escalation. Iran's parliamentary shipping-restriction bill — which would bar US/Israeli vessels and fine carriers of Israel-linked cargo up to 20% of cargo value — cleared its committee unanimously but still needs a full floor vote, Guardian Council review, and presidential signature; no floor vote yet. No new attack was confirmed overnight beyond Saturday's ADNOC tanker strike and Sunday's Houthi refinery/port attacks already reported.
- **Market effect:** Brent rose to ~$84.18-84.46 (+0.75-0.9%) Monday, extending Friday's gain and now roughly 16% above pre-war levels; WTI was little changed to slightly softer (~$78-79). Shipping disruption remains historic — only 8-15 vessels crossed the Strait August 4-6 versus roughly 130 pre-war. Separately, the Pentagon gave defense contractors a 21-day deadline (issued Aug 8) to accelerate munitions output after Patriot interceptor stockpiles fell roughly 65% from pre-war levels — a sign of US operational strain that shapes the de-escalation calculus even though it isn't directly oil-price-moving.
- **Concept:** A "final stages" deal headline and a *widening* war-risk premium can be true at the same time, because they're pricing different things. The Oman corridor is a narrow, technical fix — safe transit coordinates for specific traffic — while Iran's reopening conditions are a maximalist political ask that functions, in practice, as a way of saying the Strait stays constrained indefinitely. The market is right to treat these as separate trades: a signed corridor deal would ease *some* shipping friction without touching the structural risk premium at all, since that premium is priced off the reopening conditions, not the corridor.

### 2.3 ai-capex — AI capex cycle — STRENGTHENING (TSMC reinforces the bull case; memory pricing shows the first real crack)
- **What changed:** Two threads, pulling in the same direction but with one new wrinkle. TSMC's July revenue hit a record NT$467.6bn (+44.7% y/y, +5.6% m/m) — a seventh straight month of strong growth — and management raised FY2026 revenue growth guidance to "slightly above 40%" and 2026 capex to $60-64bn (from $52-56bn), with HPC/AI now 66% of revenue; TSMC also confirmed an additional $100bn US investment for Arizona capacity. That's the cleanest read yet that AI chip demand isn't cooling at the logic-chip level. On memory, though, Asia's Monday session showed stabilization rather than a rebound — Kospi +0.65%, SK Hynix roughly flat after opening up more than 3% — and Goldman/UBS both defended the sector over the weekend (Goldman calling Friday's selloff "ETF/leveraged-fund deleveraging," not a fundamentals shift). But the actual pricing data has a real wrinkle: SK Hynix's DRAM pricing rose ~30% q/q against Goldman's own ~39% model, and Samsung's rose >40% against a ~48% estimate — the first sign that memory pricing, not just sentiment, might be running behind the bull case, which Motley Fool flagged as a read-through risk for Micron's report next month.
- **Market effect:** Kospi +0.65% to 6,299.66, Kosdaq +7%; SK Hynix roughly flat, Samsung -0.43%. US chip names were softer in Monday premarket (Nvidia flagged as a drag on Nasdaq futures). Next reads: Super Micro Computer's FQ4 earnings call Tuesday 11 August (5pm ET) — consensus EPS $0.96, revenue $11.55bn, against a prelim 8-K already flagging over $60bn in Q4 orders — then Applied Materials Thursday.
- **Concept:** "Beat" means different things at different points in a supply chain, and that's exactly what's playing out here. TSMC's numbers say demand for AI compute is still accelerating — that's a volume signal. The memory-pricing miss is a *price* signal, and it's a subtler one: DRAM prices still rose sharply, just less than the sell-side's most bullish models expected. A volume beat and a price miss can coexist when supply is catching up to demand faster in one sub-market (memory) than another (logic/foundry) — which is why this doesn't kill the AI-capex thesis, but it does mean the easiest, most bullish version of the memory story just got a little harder to defend.

### 2.4 bond-regime — Bond market regime — TURNING (yields and the dollar firm on oil-inflation repricing, breaking last week's decline)
- **What changed:** The same Hormuz-deal doubt that moved hike odds (→2.1) pushed the 10-year yield up more than 4bp intraday to ~4.70% and firmed the dollar index modestly to ~99.65-99.80, reversing part of last week's post-payrolls decline. - **Market effect:** No dramatic cross-asset break — this reads as a partial, contained reversal rather than a new regime. US equity futures barely moved (S&P +0.07% premarket), suggesting equities haven't yet decided whether to treat the oil move as an inflation threat or noise.
- **Concept:** When yields, the dollar, and volatility all tick up together off a geopolitical (not domestic-data) trigger, it's usually the market repricing a *tail risk* rather than a base case — inflation expectations shifting a little at the margin, not a wholesale change in the growth-and-rates outlook. The real test of whether this is noise or the start of something durable is Wednesday's CPI: a hot print would turn today's modest, contained move into a much sharper one, because it would confirm the oil-driven inflation channel the market is only tentatively pricing right now.

## 5. Deep Dive — GameStop Weighs Pulling Its $56bn Hostile Bid for eBay in Favor of a Partnership

**5.1 What happened:** Bloomberg reported Monday that [[GameStop]] CEO Ryan Cohen is weighing withdrawing GameStop's $56bn cash-and-stock takeover offer for [[eBay]] — a $125/share bid backed by a $20bn TD Securities financing commitment plus roughly $9bn cash — which eBay's board rejected in May as "neither credible nor attractive." Cohen is reportedly now pitching a partnership or joint venture instead: leveraging GameStop's roughly 1,600 US stores for authentication, returns, and fulfillment on eBay's marketplace, with trading cards and collectibles as the initial wedge, while still seeking board representation. GameStop has built a 9.8% stake in eBay, making it one of the company's largest shareholders. ([Bloomberg](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-10/gamestop-s-ryan-cohen-weighs-pulling-56-billion-ebay-offer), [CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/05/12/ebay-rejects-gamestops-takeover-ryan-cohen.html))

**5.2 Why it matters:** This is a real-time case study in activist-investor mechanics: when a hostile bid gets rejected and the financing commitment behind it starts to look like a stretch relative to GameStop's own balance sheet, the credible next move is often to trade the "control" premium for an operational partnership that still monetizes the stake and the strategic logic — physical retail plus authentication expertise bolted onto an online marketplace, aimed at the same collectibles/trading-card category Amazon has been circling. It's also a live test of how much leverage a ~10% stake actually buys an activist once the market has already priced in "no full takeover" — GameStop still needs eBay's board to agree to *something*, and a JV pitch with board-seat demands is a different negotiation than a hostile tender offer.

**5.3 What's priced in / what to monitor:** eBay's board has already signaled skepticism once (the May rejection), so the market's read on a JV pitch will hinge on deal terms, not the headline. Watch for: whether GameStop formally withdraws the tender offer or lets it lapse, what specific commercial terms (revenue share, store-network access, board seats) get proposed, and whether eBay's board treats this as a genuine olive branch or as Cohen quietly climbing down from an offer the financing never quite supported. Commentary (Fortune) has already drawn parallels to past retail-tech merger disasters — worth tracking whether that skepticism proves right.

## 6. Idea Lab — TSMC (NYSE: TSM / TWSE: 2330)

Today's cleanest AI-capex data point: a record month, raised guidance, and a bigger capex budget, on the same day memory-chip pricing showed its first real crack (→2.3).

**(a) How it actually makes money:** [[TSMC]] is a pure-play semiconductor foundry — it doesn't design its own chips, it manufactures them under contract for fabless customers (Apple, Nvidia, AMD, Qualcomm and others) on advanced process nodes. Revenue is wafers shipped times average selling price, segmented by node and end-market. July revenue hit a record NT$467.6bn (+44.7% y/y), the seventh straight month of strong growth, with HPC/AI now 66% of revenue — up from a much smaller share a few years ago. The KPIs that matter: (1) HPC/AI revenue mix, the cleanest proxy for how exposed TSMC is to the AI-capex cycle versus legacy smartphone/PC demand; (2) advanced-node (3nm/5nm) share of wafer revenue, since newer nodes carry meaningfully higher ASPs and margins; (3) capex versus gross margin trajectory — TSMC just raised 2026 capex to $60-64bn (from $52-56bn) and committed an additional $100bn to Arizona capacity, so the read-through for investors is whether pricing power keeps absorbing that spend or margins start to compress under it.

> [!bullish]
> **Bull in one line:** TSMC is the irreplaceable chokepoint for advanced AI chips, and a seventh straight month of record, guidance-raising revenue says demand isn't cooling even as sentiment wobbles elsewhere in the supply chain.

> [!bearish]
> **Bear in one line:** TSMC just raised its own capex bet to $60-64bn on the same week memory pricing came in below the Street's most bullish models — if that pricing softness is an early demand signal rather than noise, TSMC has the largest, least flexible capital base in the industry to get caught leaning the wrong way.

**(c) One question for a differentiated view:** How much of TSMC's FY2026 revenue growth is coming from advanced-node price/mix (higher ASPs on 3nm and below) versus pure wafer-volume growth — and does that decomposition look different once you model a scenario where one or two hyperscalers actually slow AI capex, the way this week's memory-pricing miss hints could eventually happen? Learning tool, not a recommendation. ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/08/10/tsmc-revenue-surge-ai-chip-big-tech.html))

Is this the first real crack in the memory supercycle bull case, or noise from a couple of estimates that were simply too high?** One side: Goldman had modeled ~39% q/q DRAM pricing growth for SK Hynix and got ~30%; Morningstar modeled >48% for Samsung and got >40% — two independent misses against the *same* bullish framework is a pattern, not a coincidence, and Motley Fool's read is that HBM4 shipments coming in light plus long-term contracts pulling demand forward could mean 2026's pricing strength borrowed from 2027. ## 11. Journal Seed

The sharpest thing to form a view on today is the gap between TSMC's volume signal and memory's price signal: TSMC's record month says AI-chip demand is still accelerating, but SK Hynix and Samsung's DRAM pricing coming in below the Street's most bullish models is the first crack in the *memory-specific* version of that story. Question worth sitting with: if HBM4 shipments really are running light and long-term contracts are pulling 2027 demand into 2026, does that stay contained to memory — where supply can catch up fastest — or is it an early tremor that eventually reaches the logic/foundry names like TSMC that just bet another $60bn+ on the opposite bet?
