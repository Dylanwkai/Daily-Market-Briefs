## 2. Theme Tracker

### 2.1 fed-path — US Fed path & inflation — STABLE, front end calm even as the back end breaks
- **What changed:** July industrial production rose 0.2% (a touch below the 0.3% expected, but a second straight monthly gain), and FOMC minutes from the 28-29 July meeting — the meeting that produced a rare three-way regional-president dissent for a hike — land Wednesday. September hike odds sit in the 30-40% range depending on the source, essentially where they were Monday: modestly elevated versus two weeks ago's trough, nowhere near the 55-67% range from early August.
- **Market effect:** 2-year yield ~4.19% (vs 4.156% Monday, a small uptick); 10-year ~4.70%. Both barely moved versus the 30-year's 19-year-high break (→2.4) — the front end is pricing policy expectations that haven't actually shifted much, while the long end is doing something else entirely.
- **Concept:** The 2-year yield is almost a direct read of what traders expect the Fed funds rate to average over the next two years, so if near-term hike odds are flat, the 2-year should be flat too — and it roughly is. When the 30-year moves several times as much as the 2-year on the same day, that's the market telling you the story isn't about the Fed's next move at all — it's about something the front end doesn't price: how much extra yield investors need to hold government debt for three decades. Full detail Deep Dive (5).

### 2.2 hormuz-oil — Hormuz / Iran war — DEADLOCKED at the top, but genuine progress on the one working channel
- **What changed:** The 60-day US-Iran MOU's deadline passed Monday with the core dispute — management of the Strait and the fate of frozen Iranian funds — unresolved. Trump repeated his threat to bomb Oman "if it gets in the way," the second time he's aimed this specific threat at Washington's own mediator rather than at Iran. - **Concept:** Markets reprice hardest on the first break from an assumed status quo — that's why Monday's deadline lapsing moved oil 2.6% while today's pile of both threatening and conciliatory headlines moved it almost nothing. Once a market has already adjusted its baseline assumption (no deal is imminent), subsequent headlines just add texture to a picture it's already priced, unless one of them is itself a new hard fact (a signed agreement, or an actual strike).

- **Concept:** "The AI trade" isn't one trade — it's a supply-side story (are chips actually scarce and mispriced today?) bolted onto a financing-side story (is the capital funding the buildout sound?). They're now moving in opposite directions on the same day: memory names ripping on real, current pricing power, financing names widening on balance-sheet risk that's several steps removed from this quarter's cash flows. Conflating the two — treating Kioxia's rally and Oracle's downgrade as the same signal — misses that one is a fundamentals story and the other is a leverage story.

### 2.4 bond-regime — Bond market regime — ESCALATING sharply, the dominant cross-asset driver today
- **What changed:** The US 30-year Treasury yield broke above 5.31%, its highest level since 2007 — nearly two decades. Unlike the front-end moves that have driven this theme in prior sessions, this one isn't about near-term Fed expectations (→2.1): it's deficit-issuance supply meeting doubts about new Fed Chair Kevin Warsh's inflation credibility after a press conference where he wouldn't commit to PCE as the Fed's target measure. Japan's 10-year JGB hit a three-decade high near 2.95% the same day, on its own inflation and BOJ-hike dynamics — meaning this is now a global long-end story, not a US-specific one. Full detail Deep Dive (5).
- **Market effect:** S&P 500 -0.5% to 7,745; Microsoft -3.0%, Meta -3.5% (long-duration growth names hit hardest by a higher discount rate); DXY rebounded to ~99.7 from a two-month low — higher yields and safe-haven demand both supporting the dollar even as the week's earlier dollar-weakness narrative (on softer hike odds) hasn't reversed at the front end.
- **Concept:** Term premium is the extra yield investors demand to hold long-dated debt instead of rolling short-term bills — compensation for the risk that inflation, deficits, or policy credibility deteriorate over a 30-year horizon. It's a different animal from rate expectations: the Fed sets the short end directly, but the long end is a vote by bond investors on the government's and the central bank's long-run trustworthiness. A 30-year yield rising sharply while the 2-year barely moves (→2.1) is textbook term-premium expansion, and it's exactly the kind of move that doesn't get fixed by a rate cut — only by convincing the bond market the long-run picture is sound.

## 5. Deep Dive — The 30-Year Treasury Yield Breaks to a 19-Year High

**5.1 What happened:** The US 30-year Treasury yield climbed above 5.31% Monday, its highest level since July 2007 — a milestone last seen before the 2008 financial crisis, when the long bond spent most of the following decade-and-a-half below 3%. The move came alongside, but is mechanically distinct from, the Hormuz and Fed-path stories: near-term hike odds barely budged and the 2-year yield rose only modestly, meaning this wasn't the market pricing a more hawkish Fed. Instead, three forces are compounding: heavy Treasury issuance to fund persistent budget deficits (more supply requires a better price to clear); inflation that keeps proving stickier than hoped, with tariffs and energy costs both still live; and specific doubts about new Fed Chair Kevin Warsh, who took office in May and whose July press conference — where he wouldn't commit to PCE as the Fed's preferred gauge and stated the Fed isn't "constrained by market prices" — left the bond market openly questioning his inflation-fighting resolve rather than reassured by it. Japan's 10-year JGB hit its own three-decade high (~2.95%, first since 1996) the same day, on a parallel mix of inflation and BOJ-hike dynamics — evidence this is a global, not US-specific, long-end phenomenon. ([Bloomberg](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-17/us-bond-selloff-drives-30-year-yields-to-the-highest-since-2007), [CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/08/17/treasury-yields-federal-reserve-fomc-minutes.html), [Business Recorder](https://www.brecorder.com/news/40435336/japans-10-year-government-bond-yield-rises-to-three-decade-peak))

**5.2 Why it matters:** This is already a Main Street story, not just a Wall Street one. The average 30-year mortgage rate has climbed to roughly 6.7-6.8%, up more than 50 basis points since the Iran war began in late February, and on a $340,000 loan, the difference between 6.0% and 6.6% adds about $140 a month — over $50,000 in extra interest across the life of the loan. For equities, a higher long-end discount rate hits long-duration growth names hardest, which is exactly what happened Monday (Microsoft -3.0%, Meta -3.5%) — a different transmission channel than the Fed-hike-odds story that's dominated most of this theme's coverage so far. The dollar's rebound off a two-month low is also notable: it's being driven by yield differentials and safe-haven demand simultaneously, a combination that can persist even while the market's near-term Fed view stays roughly unchanged.

**5.3 What's priced in / what to monitor:** The move already reflects real deficit and credibility concerns, but it's not clear the market has fully processed how Warsh intends to address them. Three things to watch: (1) Wednesday's FOMC minutes, for any language on the framework review or long-run inflation strategy that might either reassure or further spook the long end; (2) demand at the next 30-year auction (bid-to-cover, indirect bidder share) as a direct read on whether foreign and institutional buyers are still willing to absorb this issuance at these yields; (3) Warsh's first major speech as Fed Chair at Jackson Hole (27-29 August), where he's expected to lay out a review of the Fed's 2020 average-inflation-targeting framework and may signal a return to strict 2% targeting — the single clearest opportunity so far for him to either rebuild or further erode the credibility the bond market is currently pricing him short of.

## 6. Trade Floor — Two Open Tensions

**6.1 FICC/Rates — The 30-year yield's break to a 19-year high happened with the front end of the curve barely moving. Is this a durable term-premium repricing that persists until Warsh proves his inflation-fighting credibility at Jackson Hole, or is it a temporary overshoot that partially reverses once fiscal-year Treasury issuance patterns normalize and the market gets more data on where inflation is actually heading?** One side: deficits aren't going away, Warsh's own comments raised rather than settled doubts about his framework, and a genuine credibility gap between a new Fed chair and bond investors historically takes months, not days, to close — arguing this holds or extends into Jackson Hole. Other side: the 2-year and 10-year both moved far less than the 30-year, which is also consistent with a technical, issuance-driven supply/demand imbalance at the long end that could unwind quickly once a strong 30-year auction or a dovish framework signal removes the immediate overhang. *Settling datapoint: demand metrics (bid-to-cover, indirect bidder share) at the next 30-year Treasury auction, and whether Warsh's Jackson Hole speech reassures or further unsettles the market on framework and credibility.*

**6.2 Equity — Memory names are rallying hard on real DRAM/NAND pricing power (Kioxia +15%, SK Hynix +8%, Micron +4.1%) while AI-financing credit spreads simultaneously hit fresh records across Oracle, Nvidia, Alphabet and SpaceX. Is the memory-pricing surge a genuinely separate, fundamentals-driven story that can keep running even if AI-financing credit stress worsens, or is the entire AI complex — memory suppliers included — ultimately hostage to the same capex cycle that the credit market is now visibly worried about funding?** One side: memory scarcity is a real, physical supply constraint (fab capacity takes years to add) that's showing up in actual sold-out order books and price increases today, largely independent of how any single hyperscaler finances its data-center buildout. Other side: memory demand is itself downstream of the same AI capex the credit market is stress-testing — if financing conditions genuinely tighten enough to slow hyperscaler capex growth, the "sold-out" memory market could loosen quickly, since much of current demand is forward-looking capacity reservation, not just current consumption. *Settling datapoint: Nvidia's 26 August earnings call and capex guidance, and whether Micron's or Kioxia's next quarterly pricing commentary confirms the upcycle extends into 2027 or starts flagging moderation.*

## 7. Idea Lab — [[Kioxia Holdings]] (TSE: 285A)

*Rotation from shipping/tankers (Frontline, yesterday) to Japanese semiconductors — Kioxia sits at the center of today's NAND-pricing story (→1.4, 2.3, 6.2) and, despite having briefly overtaken Toyota as Japan's most valuable listed company, remains genuinely under-covered by Western retail and institutional research relative to Samsung, SK Hynix or Micron.*

**(a) How it actually makes money:** Kioxia is Japan's largest NAND flash memory maker (flash memory being the non-volatile storage chip technology used in SSDs and enterprise AI storage, as distinct from the DRAM that Micron and SK Hynix also produce), operating through a decades-old joint-venture manufacturing arrangement with Western Digital's flash business. Its economics are driven almost entirely by the memory pricing cycle rather than by diversified end-markets. The three KPIs that matter: (1) NAND ASP (average selling price) trends — the company's June-quarter revenue and operating margin jumped 76% and over 1,200 basis points sequentially to ¥1.8tn and 72% op-margin purely on pricing, with January-March 2026 revenue already up 189% y/y; (2) bit shipment growth and fab utilization — a proxy for whether demand is being met by supply or genuinely outstripping it; (3) capital discipline — Kioxia's management has explicitly tied its bullish guidance to keeping new capacity additions measured even as prices spike, the opposite instinct from prior memory cycles that famously overbuilt into a price collapse. ([Morningstar](https://www.morningstar.com/company-reports/1482498-kioxia-earnings-robust-results-as-nand-pricing-significantly-exceeds-guidance-fair-value-up-165), [TradingKey](https://www.tradingkey.com/analysis/stocks/more/262066743-kioxia-quarterly-profit-surged-revenue-fell-short-expectations-memory-chips-projected-support-second-quarter-revenue-growth-tradingkey))

> [!bullish]
> **Bull in one line:** NAND pricing is guided up another 30-40% this quarter on genuine capacity scarcity, Kioxia's own outlook calls for prices to keep rising into Q3, and today's 15% single-day jump shows the market still treats fresh pricing confirmation as a reason to re-rate the stock, not to worry it's peaked.

> [!bearish]
> **Bear in one line:** Kioxia's own late-July guidance already spooked the market once — with the stock briefly plunging on signs price hikes might be moderating — and a name up roughly 20x over the past year, facing an active ITC patent investigation and rising Chinese NAND competition, is priced for the upcycle to be closer to permanent than cyclical.

**(c) One question for a differentiated view:** Kioxia's late-July outlook already gave the market one scare about moderating price hikes, before today's KeyBanc-driven NAND guidance reaccelerated the story entirely. Is that late-July wobble the start of the normal cycle-turn that memory investors should have been watching for, or was it simply conservative company guidance getting overtaken by stronger-than-expected end demand — and if it's the latter, how would you distinguish a memory maker under-guiding out of caution from one that's genuinely running out of room to raise prices further? ## 11. Journal Seed

Today's most worth-forming-a-view-on development isn't Hormuz — it's that the bond market moved on Fed credibility without the Fed actually doing anything new. The 30-year broke to a 19-year high while near-term hike odds stayed roughly flat, which means investors are pricing a multi-decade judgment about deficits and a five-month-old Fed chair, not next month's meeting. Sharp question: if Warsh's Jackson Hole speech is genuinely hawkish on the framework — a real break from average-inflation-targeting — does that calm the long end by restoring credibility, or does it spook it further by confirming the Fed sees inflation risk as being at least as serious as bond investors already fear?
